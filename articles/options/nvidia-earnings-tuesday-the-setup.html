Nvidia Earnings Tuesday: The Setup, the History, and What to Watch

November 17, 2026 · By Hedgewiz · Originally published on Substack

Nvidia reports Tuesday after the close, with the earnings call at 5:00 PM ET. Consensus is around $2.47 per share on roughly $109 billion of revenue. The stock closed Monday at $156.14, down 4.1% on the day after shedding nearly 13% last week. It is a long way from the $243.37 high of the past year — and about $7 above the $148.80 low.

Options are pricing an ~8.9% move for Wednesday — the market's bar is a swing to roughly $142 or $170 either way. That is the richest per-day implied move in our five-year sample: the history is mostly two-day expiries, and this one is one day.

There is a lot of noise around this report. Jensen Huang has resigned, CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO, and while outside voices have pushed for Huang's reinstatement, reports indicate the board is not considering bringing him back. The Justice Department has a criminal investigation open, and the Treasury Secretary has ordered the board to answer questions on export controls within ten days. None of that changes the numbers. It changes how the market hears them.

We looked at every Nvidia earnings report in our five-year backtest — sixteen of them, plus three more that a data filter left out. The honest picture is thinner than the headline. The trade in the backtest is a strangle, betting both ways: assume you bought a call and a put at the close before earnings, and closed both at the close on reaction day.

Across the sixteen, the average outcome in our backtest was +$349 per contract — and that number is not statistically significant. It is carried almost entirely by one report: May 2023 made +$5,118, which is 91% of all the profit in the set. Take the three excluded reports back in — all three lost — and the average across all nineteen falls to +$27. Only five of the sixteen actually made money.

November is the worst month in the sample. Nvidia has reported in November four times, and all four lost, for a combined −$4,131. The average absolute move in those four was just 1.94%, against a 5.96% median across all sixteen. No November report ever moved even half the ~8.9% the market is pricing this time. The worst single loss in the set is a November report — November 2023, about −$1,875 — which also had the lowest implied volatility and the lowest VIX of any report in the sample. Cheap premium did not save it: the stock moved only −2.56%.

Direction has flipped, too. In the first eight reports the stock rose six times. In the last eight it rose twice and fell six times.

One reaction day in four reverses its opening gap — and that includes the two most recent reports. In February 2025 the stock opened up 2.8% and closed down 8.6%. In November 2025 it opened up 5.1% and closed down 3.2%. The close is what counts, not the open: only once in sixteen did the intraday peak clear the priced-in move while the close did not. If you are watching Wednesday, watch the close, not the open.

A bigger implied move has not predicted a bigger realized move for Nvidia. Across the sixteen, the correlation between the implied move and the actual move is barely above zero (r = 0.15). Expensive options have not meant bigger moves — they have meant more expensive options.

This report sits outside the sample. The stock is 27% below its 50-day moving average — the lowest reading of any report in the set — and 23% below its 200-day average. Entries below the 50-day are 0-for-3 in the sample. The prior week's return, −12.6%, is also the lowest in the sample. Implied volatility near 148% is the richest per-day reading in the set, and the VIX at 22.9 is the third-highest. This is the first report in the sample where the options expire on the reaction day itself, so a small move leaves less cushion than it did historically, when the options still had a day of time value left.

None of that says Wednesday disappoints. It says the history you would lean on does not contain a setup like this one.

Fade the gap; watch the close. One reaction day in four reverses its opening gap, and the two latest both did — February 2025 swung 11.4 points from open to close, November 2025 swung 8.3. The recent pattern is an opening move that does not survive the day. Whatever Wednesday opens with, the close is the number that has mattered in fifteen of sixteen reports.

November's base case is a small move. Four November reports, four small ones: the biggest was 3.20%, the average 1.94%. In the two clean Novembers, the market priced 6.9% and 7.2% and got 2.6% and 3.2% — realized ran at roughly 40% of implied. A quiet Wednesday is not the surprise outcome in November; it is the usual one.

The trend into the earnings is down. The stock is 27% below its 50-day average, the furthest below in the sample, and coming off its worst week into a report (−12.6%). Entries below the 50-day are 0-for-3, and the only comparable entry — May 2022, 21% below its 50-day — reversed intraday and finished the day barely changed. The last eight reports lean down (up 2, down 6). Nothing here says chase strength into the earnings.

Expensive options are not a forecast. The four highest-IV entries in the sample went 1-for-4, and the four lowest-IV entries also went 1-for-4. The largest implied move in the sample (August 2024, 10.9%) produced a −6.35% day; the two biggest actual moves came on middling implied moves. What the market charges for the move tells you what it costs, not how far the stock goes.

What would change the read: a close beyond the priced-in band, roughly $142 to $170. Only 4 of 16 closes cleared 8.9%, and none of the last eight did — the last eight averaged a 4.7% absolute move. A move of that size would be the exception in this sample, and in November it has never happened.

Three things. First, the gap versus the close — if it opens up and fades, that is now the pattern, not the exception. Second, the priced-in band around $142 to $170: inside it is where history says Wednesday usually lands. Third, the call at 5 PM — guidance and the export-control questions will matter more than the quarter itself, and the interim-CEO situation makes the tone of that call worth listening to.

The broad lesson from sixteen reports is simple: the outcomes have always been lopsided. Most of the time the move is small. Once in a while it is very large — and in November, it has never been.
