Selling Premium: How Collecting Time Decay Works, and the Risk That Comes With It

November 21, 2026 · By Hedgewiz · Options education

Most people meet options as buyers: you pay a premium, you get a right, and you hope the stock moves your way. But every option bought is also sold by someone — and the seller's side works very differently. The seller collects money up front, and then time itself starts working in their favour. That is the appeal of selling premium — and, if you do not respect the risk, the trap.

What the seller is actually selling

When you sell an option, you are selling a right and taking on an obligation. Sell a call, and you agree to sell shares at the strike price if the buyer asks. Sell a put, and you agree to buy shares at the strike price if the buyer asks. In return, you collect the premium immediately, in cash. It is yours to keep if the option expires worthless.

The seller's best outcome is boring: nothing much happens. If the stock stays put or drifts without crossing the strike, the option decays and expires worthless, and the seller keeps the full premium. The buyer, by contrast, needs a real move to make money.

Time decay: the wind at the seller's back

An option's premium is made of two parts: intrinsic value, the part already in the money, and time value, the price of the chance that the option might become worth more before expiry. Time value is what the seller is really collecting. Time value shrinks every single day, and reaches zero on the expiry date.

Traders call the daily rate of that shrinkage theta. You do not need the maths. Every day without a big move, the option is worth a little less, because there is one less day for the big move to happen. The seller gains from that shrinkage; the buyer loses from it.

Why the win rate can be high

A premium seller can win most of the time. An option sold well out of the money only loses if the stock makes an unusually large move before expiry — and large moves are, by definition, unusual. So a seller can enjoy a long run of small, steady wins.

But win rate is not the same as profit. The seller's wins are capped at the premium collected, which is usually modest. The losses, when they come, can be several times larger than a single win. One sharp move can wipe out many months of collected premium in a single afternoon. Winning nine times out of ten means nothing if the one loss is twelve times the size of a win. A high win rate feels like safety, and it is not.

Defined risk

There is an important distinction between two kinds of premium selling. Selling a call or a put on its own — "naked" selling — leaves the risk open-ended. If the stock moves far enough against you, the loss keeps growing with no natural ceiling, and a naked call has no theoretical limit at all. That is a risk most individuals should not take with real money.

The safer form is defined-risk selling: combining the option you sell with another option you buy, so your maximum loss is fixed and known before you enter. In a spread, you sell one option and buy another at a further strike; the bought option costs some premium, but it caps your loss at the distance between the strikes. You know the worst case on day one, in dollars.

Position sizing

The single most important discipline in selling premium is sizing: how much you risk on any one position, relative to your account. Because the losses are rare but large, a position that is too big can end the whole endeavour on a single bad day. Size each position so that even a full loss — the worst case, with defined risk — is a small fraction of your account, an amount you can absorb and keep going after.

Sizing also means limiting how much is committed at any one time. Twenty small positions can all be hit by one market-wide shock — a sharp fall hurts every put you sold, a sharp rally every call. The seller's edge is small and steady; it only works if you survive long enough to collect it many times.

When selling premium works, and when it does not

Selling premium works best in calm, range-bound conditions, where the underlying moves less than the market expected. It struggles badly in sharp, sudden moves — the kind that follow unexpected news, earnings announcements, or market-wide shocks — because those are exactly the moves that push options deep into the money. A seller who collects premium week after week in a quiet market can give much of it back in one volatile week.

That is not a reason to avoid selling premium. It is a reason to go in with your eyes open: respect the rare big loss, cap your risk, size so a bad day is survivable, and never confuse a long winning streak with safety.

Selling options can lose more than the premium collected, and naked selling can lose far more. Know your maximum loss, use defined-risk structures, and size positions so one bad move cannot damage your account. Paper trading first is a sensible step.
